How the model works

In the simple modes the hip is pinned in place. The thigh swings as a smooth sine wave through the arc you set, once forward and once back each stride. In Bend, then straighten mode the knee goes smoothly from straight to the maximum bend and back to straight during the forward swing (starting and ending at rest, with no sudden change in torque), and stays straight on the backward swing.

For each of 1,440 instants per stride the simulator solves the equations of motion for a double pendulum with real segment masses and inertias:

Q₁ = M₁₁θ̈ + M₁₂ψ̈ + m₂L₁c₂ sin(θ−ψ) ψ̇² + (m₁c₁ + m₂L₁) g sin θ
Q₂ = M₁₂θ̈ + M₂₂ψ̈ − m₂L₁c₂ sin(θ−ψ) θ̇² + m₂c₂ g sin ψ
knee torque = Q₂,   hip torque = Q₁ + Q₂
power = torque × joint angular velocity

θ is the thigh angle and ψ the lower-leg angle, both from vertical. Positive power is muscle work; negative power is braking, assumed lost unless tendon return is turned up. Both legs are counted.

Realistic stride

The foot lands heel first, ahead of the hip, with the toes up and the knee bent 10°. If the leg would lock straight just after landing (a foot landing almost under the hip, or a very short contact), it lands with more knee bend instead. It slaps flat, stays planted while the body runs over it, then the heel lifts and the foot rolls over the ball onto the toes. If the leg needs more reach, the toes roll up too before the foot leaves the ground. Between steps the body flies on a free-fall arc.

The ground force follows a spring-mass pattern sized so it supports body weight over each step. The body's height comes straight from that force, so the drawn hip, the swinging leg and the planted leg all share one motion. The hip's vertical acceleration acts on the swinging leg as extra or reduced gravity: about 2–3 g while the other foot is pushing, zero in the air. The swing phase and the stance phase are added up separately.

On the ground the knee bend is not set: it is whatever the leg needs to reach from the hip down to the planted foot, so it bends as the body sinks onto the leg. The foot slaps flat at the rate that gives a typical knee speed just after landing, and the heel lifts just enough to keep the knee from straightening much past 22° before toe-off. In the air the thigh swings forward to a peak that grows with speed as measured in runners (33° from vertical at 3 m/s, 51° at 6 m/s), then pulls back before landing, and the knee follows typical running values: a deep bend that grows with speed, then nearly straight before landing. If the foot would scuff the ground on its way in to landing (mostly at slow speeds and high cadences), the thigh swings a little further forward before pulling back, which lifts the foot. You can override the swing knee bend; the small chart under the control reruns the whole stride at every bend from 40° to 140°.

Stance: the ground force acts at the centre of pressure, which sits under the heel as the foot lowers, moves to the ball while the foot is flat, moves out along the flat toes to the tip while the heel lifts, and stays on the tip while the toes roll. The force points from there at the hip. Its timing within the contact shifts earlier or later so that braking and propulsion cancel over each contact (steady speed). Contact times where no timing can do that are left out of the contact-time range; if no contact time works with the chosen landing point, the page says so and tilts the force off the hip instead. Hip, knee, ankle and toe-joint moments come from that force, treating the leg as weightless while it carries the body; the leg's own weight and inertia are small next to 2–3 body weights. Because the force passes through the hip, the hip does no work during contact in this model: all of its work is in the swing. Joint power is moment times joint angular speed. Summed over the joints it equals the power the ground force delivers to the body, and over each step the body ends with the energy it started with.

Assumptions

  • Average bodies from the CDC (2021–2023): man 175.0 cm, 90.3 kg; woman 161.3 cm, 77.9 kg.
  • Segment masses, centres of mass and inertias from de Leva (1996); segment lengths from Winter's ratios of height.
  • Lower leg and foot move as one rigid piece; the foot's mass sits just below the ankle.
  • Cadence counts steps per minute, both feet. Each leg makes cadence ÷ 120 strides per second.
  • Simple modes: the hip does not move and nothing touches the ground.
  • Typical values for this speed (the arrows under the sliders) come from measured runners. From runners at 3.1–10 m/s (Clark, Meng & Stearne 2020): the thigh's angle at touchdown sets the landing point (about 18–23° from 3 to 6 m/s), and contact time is 0.78 × speed−0.89 seconds, scaled with height (about 0.95–1.0 leg lengths of travel). Cadence is 133.9 + 10.9 × speed steps per minute, fitted to 28 recreational runners at 2.5–4.5 m/s (Fukuchi et al. 2017). Click an arrow to use that value. At typical cadences the model reaches the typical contact time from 2.5 m/s up (at 2 m/s it reaches 366 ms of 421). At faster-than-typical cadences at slow speeds there isn't room between steps, so it uses the nearest it can.
  • Foot: the heel strikes with the toes 12° up and the foot is flat by 12% of the contact. The heel starts to lift at about 55% of the contact (earlier if, after its deepest bend, the knee would otherwise straighten past 30°) and can lift up to 65°, while the pressure moves out to the toe tip. The toes (the front 30% of the foot) then roll up about their tip, up to 45°, only as far as the leg needs to reach. The toe joint mostly absorbs energy as it bends. The pelvis does not tilt or rotate.
  • The swinging leg's foot is lumped with the shin, so the ankle only appears during stance.
  • Peak forward thigh swing: 0.102 × speed + 0.273 radians from vertical (Clark, Meng & Stearne 2020, runners at 3.1–10 m/s), and at least 4° past the touchdown angle. Cadence stays where you set it; real runners raise it with speed (the cadence arrow: about 167 steps/min at 3 m/s and 199 at 6 m/s), which makes swing cost climb faster with speed than it does at a fixed cadence.
  • In stance, real tendons (especially the Achilles) store braking work and give much of it back. About 40% tendon energy return brings the whole-stride food energy in line with measured running costs (roughly 3.7–4 J per kg per metre).
  • Contact time and contact length are one setting (length = speed × time): set either, and the one you set stays put when you change speed. Setting it, or the landing point, moves the push-off point. Shorter contact means a harder push from the ground, since the same body weight has to be supported in less time.
  • Read joint shares with care. The ground force is aimed through the hip, so in this model the hip does no work while the foot is down and the knee and ankle do all of it. In real runners the force line passes a little in front of and behind the hip and the hip muscles do a good share of stance work, so here the knee and ankle look more important than they are, with peak knee moments about 1.4–1.7 times measured values and peak ankle moments 25–55% above them. Whole-stride totals are less affected than the split between joints.
  • Cadence matters too much for swing. The swing leg follows a set path, so a higher cadence means the same path in less time, and swing work climbs very steeply. Measured limb-moving work rises much more gently with step rate. The direction is right; the size of the effect is exaggerated.
  • Food energy is a rough conversion. All positive work is converted at one muscle efficiency (25% by default) and braking work is treated as free. Measured running looks far more efficient than 25% (apparent efficiencies of about 45–70% have been reported, largely thanks to tendon recoil), and braking work does cost some energy. That is why about 40% tendon return is needed to match measured costs. Comparisons between settings are more trustworthy than the absolute calories.
  • All knee motion is driven by muscle. Real runners get much of the knee bend for free from push-off and whip-like coupling with the thigh, so measured costs are lower than Bend, then straighten shows here.
